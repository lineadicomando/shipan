# The vernaculars, and two things that are not languages

## Who is reading

**Someone who does not read Chinese, in a language of their own.** That is the
premise the whole of this file follows from, and it has two halves that are
easy to hold only one of.

The first half is why hanzi and pinyin exist in the output at all: the reader
has come to a Chinese art, the things in it have Chinese names, and a name
withheld is a thing they cannot look up, quote or ask anybody about. The
second half is why nothing they *operate* is in Chinese: a glyph they cannot
pronounce is not a label, it is a picture of one.

**How many languages there are is a state and not a design.** Two today,
English and Italian; Spanish when the engine has stopped gaining boards — see
`ROADMAP.md`. Nothing here may be written as though the set were closed at
two, and the honest test of anything added to the catalogs is what a third
language would cost to write: a gloss beside a name costs one line, a
paragraph of argument costs a translator who has to follow the argument.
That asymmetry is the reason the derived half of a surface is worth building
and the written half is worth keeping short.

**English is the default and the fallback of last resort**, which is a
different claim from being the better language: a surface that cannot
determine a locale must still produce readable text, and English is what it
produces. `LOCALES` in `packages/i18n/src/locale.ts` is the list, and
`apps/web/test/docs.test.ts` holds this page to it.

## The engine does not localise

**Exactly as it does not interpret.** A calculation in `core` returns
identifiers, hanzi, pinyin and numbers and never a word in anybody's language:
a board is the same object on `/en` and on `/it`, which is what makes it
testable at all and what keeps the engine clear of a concern belonging to
whoever displays the result.

**What the rule bounds is choosing a language, not rendering one.** `labels.ts`,
`format.ts` and `prompt.ts` do produce readable text inside `core` — the table
of words a drawing is handed, the dense printing the CLI and `/api/…/text`
share, the prompt builders — and each takes a `Translator` as an argument.
They import types from `packages/i18n` and no catalog. Nothing in a
calculation negotiates a locale, reads one out of the environment or holds a
message, so the same board put through two translators comes out twice in one
shape; a function that picked its own language, or reached for a catalog to
find one, is the design error this rule is about. Rendering where the
calculation lives is what stops the CLI and the interface from naming one gate
two different ways.

**Two things in `core` do choose, and both are surfaces rather than engine.**
`cli.ts` negotiates like every other surface — `--lang`, then the environment,
then English — because a command is a surface that happens to live in this
package. And `errors.ts` renders `message` at `DEFAULT_LOCALE`, which is the
English line for logs and stack traces described under *Errors* below; what a
person reads is still `messageKey` translated where they are.

## Four kinds of string, four treatments

| | Example | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **Identifier** | `xiumen`, `tianpeng`, `zhifu` | `core`. Toneless pinyin, ASCII, never translated |
| **Hanzi** | 休門, 天蓬, 值符 | `core`. Domain data — **not** a locale |
| **Pinyin** | xiūmén, tiānpéng, zhífú | `core`. Part of the name — **not** a locale |
| **Label** | "Rest Gate" / "Porta del Riposo" | `i18n`, keyed by identifier |

The two middle rows are the ones that get argued about.

**Chinese characters are not a locale.** 休門 is not the English-to-Chinese
rendering of "Rest Gate": it is the name of the gate, and an Italian reader
and an English reader both expect to see it in the palace. Hanzi travels in
the engine's output regardless of locale; the catalog supplies only the
vernacular gloss beside it.

**A name carries its reading.** Every named thing has a `pinyin` beside its
`hanzi` — tone marks, one word, `xiūmén`. A surface that prints the hanzi
prints the reading with it, because a glyph alone is, to the reader this is
built for, a shape with no sound: unsayable, unsearchable, unaskable. The
tones are what the identifiers had to drop, and they part what the identifiers
cannot — 戊 wù from 午 wǔ, 驚門 jīngmén from 景門 jǐngmén. `test/pinyin.test.ts`
checks that no name is missing one.

**A prose message says the glyphs it shows, and the reading stands behind
them.** The engine's guarantee stops at the engine: a message is a sentence
somebody wrote, and for as long as nobody looked, about a third of the ones
carrying hanzi named a thing without saying it — 行年 in an error, 子時 in a
form, 用神 in a note. `apps/web/test/names.test.ts` is the guard, and it holds
every message a *person* reads to the reading this project gives each name:
too little marked fails, too much marked fails, and a reading standing
anywhere but immediately behind its glyphs fails. That fixed order is the
house rule and not a preference — it is what lets a reading be *found* in a
finished sentence, which is what keeps markup out of the catalogs.

**The prompts are excluded, by prefix rather than by judgement.** The reader
of a `prompt.` message is a model, and a model does not need to pronounce 命宮
to work with it; a reading there would lengthen a prompt without adding
anything read off it.

Where dropping the tone would collide in an *identifier*, and only there, the
tone number is kept: `jing1men` is 驚門 and `jing3men` is 景門.

A useful consequence of all this: the SVG drawing is largely
locale-independent, because the palaces contain hanzi. `plate` needs a locale
only for captions and legend, which keeps it a near-pure rendering package.

## What leads, and what stands beside

The premise at the top of this page does not contradict the rule just above
it; it bounds it, and this is the bound. Hanzi accompany the engine's output —
the palaces, the tables, the names of things — always beside a gloss, never
alone. Everything the reader is expected to *operate* or *decide from* leads
in their own language: buttons,
labels, legends, the values a choice offers. A control whose face is a glyph,
or an option that reads `zishi`, is one nobody can use on purpose, and a
`title` attribute does not rescue it — nobody hovers what they cannot
recognise.

The hanzi stands beside the word only where **what is named is Chinese**. 時辰
is; a day is not.

### What the rule is about, which is what bounds it

**The harm this guards against is a name printed as a shape.** A reader who
does not read Chinese meets 時辰 on a button and cannot say it, cannot type it
into a search, and cannot ask anybody about it: the control is operable only
by somebody who already knows what it says. The same harm arrives romanised —
an option reading `zishi` is a string that reader cannot place either. That is
why the rule catches both, and why a `title` does not rescue either: nobody
hovers what they cannot recognise.

**So the rule reaches a face that is a name, and stops there.** A face that
names nothing is not what it is about. An arrow, a printer, a circle, two
links of a chain: these are marks for what a control does. Nobody pronounces
them, nobody searches for them, and nothing about them is Chinese — the reader
this project is built for has met all of them in every interface they have
ever used. Asking «is there a word missing here?» of an arrow gives the rule
an errand it does not have, and the answer would be a word that says what the
arrow already says.

The test is therefore not *is the face a picture* but **is the face a name the
reader would otherwise have to be able to say**. What governs a mark instead
is that it be recognisable, and that it carry its accessible name — the word
in the reader's language, on the tooltip and to a screen reader, so that
somebody who cannot see the mark is not left with nothing. `Icon.svelte`
draws them and states that condition; the back-to-top arrow and Zi Wei's
return to the board are both marks under it.

**One control is a glyph and is still outside the rule: 雨**, in the header,
which starts and stops the rain of glyphs behind the page — see
`docs/architecture.md`. It is there as a mark and not as a name: it is drawn
from the thing it does rather than saying it, no chart, no option and no
answer is reachable through it, and nobody is expected to pronounce it. That
is the same standing the circle beside it has for how much light the page
has. What the reader operates it from is `rain.label`, in their own language,
on the tooltip and to a screen reader, with `aria-pressed` for which way it is
set. **A second glyph taken as a mark would mean the rule had been dropped
rather than bounded** — a hanzi has a reading whether or not this interface
wants one, and the exception is the one place that is set aside.

The same division governs the nav. An *act* is described, in the reader's own
language, so `nav.consult` and `nav.moments` differ between the catalogs. An
*instrument* is named, and a name does not translate: `Qi Men`, `Liu Ren`,
`Qi Zheng`, `Ba Zi`, `Zi Wei`, `Tai Yi` are the same string in both. The gloss
belongs in the heading of the page, which has room for it.

**The project's own name is cut to `shipan` in one place: the web manifest.**
Everywhere else it travels whole — `shipan` 式盤 shìpán, the three-part shape
every named thing here has, and the header's wordmark carries all of it. A
launcher is where that inverts. `shipan` and `shìpán` are the same word twice,
one of them accented; the glyph is already on the icon beside the string; and
the string sits in a title bar that truncates. What the rule exists to
prevent — a shape the reader cannot say — is precisely what is left when the
other two are dropped, so dropping them there costs the reader nothing and
saves them a name printed three times. `manifest.test.ts` holds it, so that
putting the reading back has to be done on purpose.

## Errors

`GeoError` and `ChartError` carry an English `code` (`INVALID_DATE`,
`UNKNOWN_TIMEZONE`, `INVALID_COORDINATES`, `EPHEMERIS_FAILURE`, …) plus a
`params` record. `message` is an English rendering for logs and stack traces;
a surface translates `messageKey` with `params`. Throwing localised sentences
from a pure engine reintroduces the coupling the whole design avoids.

The same holds for warnings — an ambiguous local hour, a nonexistent hour, a
polar-latitude fallback. Each is a `{ code, params }` pair, never a sentence.

Over HTTP the shape is `code` + `messageKey` + `params`. The surface
translates; nobody parses prose. See `apps/web/src/lib/server/errors.ts`.

## The catalog

No i18n framework. The message set is mostly nominal labels, and a catalog
keyed by a flat union of message keys is a `Record` and a `format` function —
what a framework would add is plural rules, gender agreement and message
syntax, none of which this set has ever needed, at the price of a runtime
dependency that must also clear AGPL compatibility. A language that needed one
of those three would be the thing that changes this, and a third language on
its own would not.

```ts
export const LOCALES = ['en', 'it'] as const;
export type Locale = (typeof LOCALES)[number];
export type MessageKey = keyof typeof en;

export const catalogs: Record<Locale, Record<MessageKey, string>> = { en, it };
```

Typing the Italian catalog as `Record<MessageKey, string>` makes a missing key
a compile error rather than a runtime fallback. A test asserts key parity in
both directions, so a stale Italian entry surfaces too. Interpolation is a
ten-line `format(template, params)`.

Dates and numbers go through `Intl.DateTimeFormat` and `Intl.NumberFormat`.
**Input formats do not follow the locale**: dates are always ISO `YYYY-MM-DD`
on every surface, so a shared URL means the same thing everywhere.

## Locale on each surface

| Surface | Negotiation |
|---|---|
| Web | path prefix `/en/…`, `/it/…`; `/` redirects on `Accept-Language`, falling back to `en`. Canonical English slugs, named for the art: `/qimen`, `/liuren`, `/bazi` |
| REST | `?lang=` query parameter, default `en`. It affects `label` fields and warning text only — identifiers, hanzi, pinyin and numbers never change |
| MCP | `lang` parameter per tool, default `en`; server instructions in English |
| CLI | `--lang`, otherwise `LC_ALL`/`LANG`/`LANGUAGE`, otherwise `en` |

The path prefix rather than a query parameter is deliberate: the interface is
public and indexable, and each language should be a distinct address. The
API keeps `lang` as a query parameter because it composes with the other chart
parameters already in the URL.

**The web manifest follows the path prefix too, and so does the page shown
when there is no network.** `/[lang]/manifest.webmanifest` is served from the
catalog of that language, because a manifest carries a name, a description and
a `lang`, and a single file in `static/` would have kept a vernacular string
somewhere that is not a catalog. `/[lang]/offline` is prerendered once per
locale from `LOCALES` for the same reason. Neither is a place a third language
has to be remembered in: both are read off the list.

## English is free in `geo`

GeoNames stores the international exonym in the primary `name` column —
`Rome`, `Munich`, `Naples`, `Paris`, `Beijing` — which is exactly what the
English locale wants, and `countryInfo.txt` likewise carries English country
names. The expensive streaming pass over `alternateNames.zip` therefore exists
for the other vernaculars alone — Italian today — and a language added later
pays only that pass, not a second dataset.

## And the source is English

Code, comments, identifiers, error codes, documentation and commit messages.
A vernacular exists in the catalogs of `packages/i18n` and nowhere else. That
includes this directory.

**With one exception, and it is inside the catalogs: a catalog argues a
wording in the language of that wording.** A comment defending the Italian
word above it is written in Italian, and the same comment in `en.ts` is
written in English, because it is defending an English one.

The reason is not symmetry. An argument about a word has to *use* the word —
«危 è l'ufficiale chiamato pericolo esattamente come 死門 è la porta chiamata
morte» is about how far the Italian noun may be pushed, and conducting it in
English would quote its own subject at every mention and reason about it in a
language where the choice does not arise. A translator opening `it.ts` to
weigh a rendering meets the case for the current one in the language they are
weighing it in; that is who the comment is for.

The bound is narrow and holds the rest of the rule up. What may be argued in a
vernacular is a **wording**: which word, how far it reaches, why not the
obvious one. Everything else a comment in a catalog might say — how a key is
built, which surface reads it, why a family exists — is English like every
other comment in this repository, because none of it is about the language it
is written beside.
